01 / Concept

Housing costs versus incomes

The course slide benchmark is under 30% of before-tax income; affordability must also account for wages and location.

High priority / Oct 23 + Oct 26

Housing affordability, explained as a real estate decision

Understand the causal chain, not just policy names. The key is who receives a housing benefit, who supplies capital, and what maintains affordability after opening.

Why homes become unaffordable

Price and rent growth outpace some household incomes when strong demand meets constraints on developable land, permitted density, approvals, labour, construction cost and financing. Incomes are unequal: a city can have high average wages and still price out teachers and service workers.

The 30% of before-tax income rule is a useful screening benchmark, not a complete welfare measure. Location also matters: a cheaper distant home can demand more spending and time on transportation.

Source: Holtermans C12 Affordable Housing, slides 2 to 9 and 24 to 25; California Darden case pp. 1 to 10; Government Policy deck, Oct 26.

Who pays versus who benefits?

Municipality / government
Changes allowable density, grants relief or subsidies, forgoes certain charges or funds housing programs.
Developer / investor
Supplies equity, takes construction and financing risks, and needs a workable revenue and cost equation.
Lender
Supplies repayable debt subject to underwriting and debt service, potentially on preferential terms.
Tenant / future owner
May receive lower rent, lower utility bills, a subsidy or better access, but not necessarily every benefit.

Analytical framework based on California case; Dream West Don Lands; Union Parts I and II; Mueller housing finance discussion.

California: unlocking supply

The case combines zoning, land scarcity, construction delays and cost pressures with demand and unequal wages. It asks which policies can increase accessible, actually built housing while protecting incumbent tenants and managing costs. Permission is not production, and new market-rate supply is not automatically permanently affordable.

Recognize: ADUs and JADUs, Section 8, rent regulation, YIMBY/NIMBY, upzoning, Vienna comparison and inclusionary feasibility.

Source: California's Affordable Housing Crisis, Darden 2022; C12 slides.

Union: preserving existing affordability

Union combines community land trust objectives, one-member-one-vote governance and community share-issuing finance. Instead of adding immediate new supply, it aims to preserve existing low rents. Part II tests the conflict when very low lawful rents cannot support the mortgage required at the seller's price.

Recognize: affordable NOAH stock; 58-unit first acquisition; rising interest rates; tenant-requested rent increases; lender constraints and preservation ethics.

Source: Ivey Union Co-operative Parts I and II, 2025. The case does not prove a final decision on Part II's second acquisition.

Financing versus NOI: A 50-year amortization can reduce annual debt payments; it does not automatically increase a building's NOI. Tenant-paid utility savings may not enter owner NOI. Check lease terms, rent restrictions and exit assumptions. Source: Dream, Union, Mueller and CAGBC/PwC.

A comparison is a decision tool

Concept connections

Every relationship includes a practical commercial real estate implication. Focus on what changes the underwriting or stakeholder decision.

Affordability policyvsPermanent affordability

A voucher, upzoning or initial subsidy can make housing accessible today without preserving below-market rent when tenants change. Long-term covenants, mission-led stewardship and enforceability address duration.

CRE example: A municipality funds a first occupancy discount, but later market resets erase the original benefit. Union seeks lasting community stewardship.

Source: California case; Union Parts I and II

Financing mechanismvsAffordability outcome

Cheap debt or tax relief changes developer cost and debt service, not necessarily rent paid by a household. Track funding conditions and who captures subsidy.

CRE example: A 50-year amortization helps debt coverage while tenants still pay market rent unless affordability conditions bind.

Source: Holtermans housing slides 20 to 23; Dream West Don Lands

Physical climate riskvsTransition risk

Physical means storm, flood, heat or other hazard damage and disruption. Transition means evolving energy policy, technology, carbon regulation, tenant demand and investment obsolescence.

CRE example: Warehouse flood insurance exposure is physical. Office gas-boiler retrofit obligations are transition exposure.

Source: Mueller Nov 16; C16 Physical Risk Overview

AdaptationvsMitigation

Adaptation reduces consequences of climate hazards. Mitigation reduces greenhouse gases. They can reinforce each other but are not identical.

CRE example: Floodproof a basement to avoid shutdown; electrify a boiler to lower operational emissions.

Source: Physical Risk slides p. 13; Kitson case

InsurancevsPhysical resilience

Insurance transfers some specified financial losses subject to exclusions, premiums, deductibles and limits. Physical resilience reduces actual damage or downtime.

CRE example: A covered retail plaza still loses tenant revenue and access if floodwater overtops drains.

Source: Link case; JLL 2025 insurance article

Green premiumvsBrown discount

Better assets might command extra pricing, but an older asset can trade lower from retrofit costs or tenant risk even without measurable above-benchmark rent for the good asset.

CRE example: An office facing mandatory future upgrades has a lower bid, while an efficient comparable trades at ordinary market rent.

Source: Mueller slides 11 to 13; CAGBC/PwC 2026

Environmental improvementvsPrivate financial return

Lower emissions are an environmental outcome. Owner NOI changes only when owners capture economic benefits. Count CapEx, financing and tenant leases before claiming IRR.

CRE example: An office tenant pays utilities, so tenant savings do not automatically increase the owner NOI.

Source: EDGE slides 5 to 6; CAGBC/PwC 2026

Building-level resiliencevsUrban-level resilience

Site hardening is necessary in some settings but roads, drainage, grid and land-use planning can determine whether a property remains usable.

CRE example: Babcock Ranch floodwater management spans a community. Link depended on drainage outside its portfolio.

Source: Kitson case; Link case

OutputvsOutcome

An output is an installed system or number of affordable units. An outcome is the verified change in household benefit or emissions.

CRE example: 100 restricted units are an output; demonstrated improved household housing security is an outcome.

Source: Dream Exhibit 6; Rob Simpson slides

Climate hazard scorevsFinancial materiality

A hazard score identifies exposure, but vulnerability, downtime, resilience and dependencies determine how much value is truly at risk.

CRE example: A mapped flood zone is less decisive than whether basement switchgear and the sole road access are protected.

Source: C16 Physical Risk Overview; BMO/ClimateFirst summary

ZCB-DesignvsZCB-Performance

Design validates a modeled project; annual performance certification requires actual operating evidence.

CRE example: A new project with ZCB-Design cannot claim measured year-one operations before occupancy.

Source: Mueller slides 47 to 57

Green taxonomy methodologyvsBuilding technical screening

Canada's October 2026 final methodology provides principles and activities, while technical criteria for buildings were still anticipated during 2027.

CRE example: A renovation plan may fit the concept of transition, but must not be described as already certified taxonomy-eligible.

Source: Mueller slides 8 to 10

Construction supply reformvsOwnership-based preservation

More zoning capacity, factory output and gentler density can improve construction supply; ownership and covenants determine who holds the rent benefits over time.

CRE example: A new market-rent fourplex adds units; Union buys existing low-rent stock and commits to stewarding it.

Source: California; UrbanToronto; Union; CCI

SCC social damagesvsOwner property cash flow

Social cost of carbon values society-wide damages of incremental CO2; investor NOI requires a real rent, fee or expense mechanism.

CRE example: A rail project averts CO2 damages but a private retail landlord cannot book SCC as rental income.

Source: RFF Social Cost of Carbon 101

Smart technology adoptionvsOperational performance

Sensors and dashboards do not automatically yield accurate data, occupant engagement or optimized HVAC.

CRE example: The Edge app check-ins fell to 1% despite its infrastructure.

Source: EDGE case; ASHB; Building Engines/BOMA
